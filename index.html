About

The AI Epistemic Impacts Initiative (EII) breaks down and shares important research on how AI affects the human mind, direct from the researchers and institutions doing the work.

For humanity to thrive in a future with advanced AI, people need to protect their ability to reason and think for themselves. AI developers and the AI safety community pay too little attention to this. EII exists to make the evidence easier to find and understand.

Each update features new work in the field, through interviews with the people behind it and plain-language summaries of what they found.

What are epistemic risks?

Epistemic risks are threats to humanity’s shared ability to form accurate beliefs, reason well and keep a healthy information environment.

EII uses the mental functions defined in the WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) as a map of which aspects of human cognition AI could harm or support.

There are three broad categories of epistemic risks, and they can compound one another.

Persuasion and Manipulation

AI can change what people believe, whether or not anyone intends it to. GPT-4 out-persuaded human debaters when it knew basic facts about its opponent. Sycophantic chatbots made users’ views more extreme and their confidence higher. Criminals have used AI-generated voice messages to impersonate senior US officials.

Cognitive Offloading

People can hand AI the work of reasoning, and lose the skills they stop using. Programmers who learned a new tool with AI help were worse at debugging afterwards. People writing essays with ChatGPT showed weaker brain connectivity than those using search or no tools. University students most often use AI for creating and analysing, the most demanding kinds of thinking.

Feedback Loops

People learn from AI and AI learns from people, which can narrow the range of ideas both draw on. Scientists using AI publish more, but science as a whole studies a narrower set of topics. Brainstorming with ChatGPT produced fewer distinct ideas. Words favoured by ChatGPT have spread into human speech in academic talks and podcasts.
