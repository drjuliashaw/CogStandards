About

The AI Epistemic Impacts Initiative (EII) seeks to understand and communicate the impact of AI on the human mind.

We think that for humanity to thrive in a future with advanced AI we need to protect our ability to reason and think for ourselves. This, we think, is a priority that AI developers and the AI safety community are paying insufficient attention to. The EII’s purpose is to change that.

Through research, meetings, and public dissemination of important findings on epistemic risks and virtues we aim to strengthen our collective cognitive security.

What are epistemic risks?

Epistemic risks are threats to humanity’s shared ability to form accurate beliefs, reason well and keep a healthy information environment.

EII uses the mental functions defined in the WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) as a map of which aspects of human cognition AI could harm or support.

There are three broad categories of epistemic risks, and they can compound one another.

Persuasion and Manipulation

AI systems already match, and in some studies exceed, human persuasiveness, for example by winning debates against human opponents. They become more persuasive as models grow more capable. State and non-state actors already use AI for political influence, economic manipulation and recruitment to terrorist organisations, and deepfakes have been used to impersonate government officials.

Harm can also happen when nobody intends it. Sycophantic models reinforce what users already believe, make their attitudes more extreme and reduce their willingness to look for evidence that they are wrong. Exposure is large. Well over a billion people use chatbots, and in the week before the 2024 UK general election, 32% of chatbot users discussed topics relevant to their vote with one.

Cognitive Offloading

Search engines changed how people recall information. AI goes further, because people can hand it the work of forming beliefs, building explanations and reasoning itself. Among university students, the most common uses of AI are creating (39.8%) and analysing (30.2%). Software engineers showed reduced debugging ability after coding with AI, and an EEG study found lower brain connectivity in people writing with AI than in people using a search engine or no tools.

The effect can feed itself. As people lose trust in their own reasoning they hand more of it over, a pattern described as “cognitive surrender”. It is not spread evenly: marginalised students are more likely to let AI do the work entirely, while students with more education use it to extend their own thinking. People whose critical thinking has been dulled in this way are also more open to manipulation.

Feedback Loops

People learn from AI, and AI learns from what people produce, including content written with AI. These loops are narrowing the range of ideas both draw on. AI tools increase individual scientists’ output and citations but narrow the set of topics that science pursues as a whole. AI-assisted brainstorming produces fewer unique ideas than human-only groups, and a study of 280,000 academic talks found ChatGPT’s typical phrasing spreading into human speech.

The same loops can also split people into separate camps, for example when a model is tuned to suit one political audience. The endpoint the paper warns of is “lock-in”, where society becomes stuck in self-referential cycles that resist correction and can no longer move towards new knowledge.

Source: AI Epistemic Risks: Emerging Mechanisms & Evidence
